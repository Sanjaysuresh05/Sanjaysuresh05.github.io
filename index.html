About

I build the hardware layer quantum computing runs on.

Over the past two years I've moved from a single transmon qubit to a 4-qubit flip-chip processor — cutting crosstalk from −37 dB to −55 dB along the way — while working inside C-DAC Bengaluru's Quantum Technology Group and CIT's Centre for Quantum Science and Technology under Dr. Md. Manirul Ali.

Lately that's stretched into two adjacent problems: an inverse-design engine that turns a target dispersive shift directly into a fabricable qubit geometry, and a machine-learning framework — built during my internship at QNu Labs — that tells real quantum randomness apart from an algorithm pretending to be random.

I'm aiming next at graduate research in superconducting quantum hardware, in a group pushing the fabrication-to-characterization loop forward.

  3. RSAF — Randomness Source Attribution Framework

    QNu Labs, SparQ Internship Program · May–Jul 2026

    Tells you not just whether a sequence looks random, but where it actually came from.

    • 64-dimensional statistical fingerprint (entropy, spectral, compression, correlation, CUSUM) extracted from 512 KB windows across 37 binary sources (~4.15 GB)
    • Five-model ensemble (CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest, Extra Trees) with isotonic calibration and hierarchical window → file → generator voting
    • Reached 81.21% mean generator-level attribution confidence, a calibrated Brier score of 0.16, and 73.2% ensemble F1
    • Python
    • CatBoost
    • XGBoost
    • LightGBM
    • scikit-learn
    • SHAP
    Repository
